===== SLIDE 8 =====
Additional Risks of Cocaine Abuse
Cocaine provides a good example of
selective tolerance
; in which increasing amounts of the drug are required to produce the desired psychological effects, but the person becomes supersensitive to the effect that produces seizures
It is likely that the risks of cocaine abuse have been underestimated. In one study, rats were allowed to press a lever that caused heroin or cocaine to be injected into their bloodstream. After one month, 90% of rats receiving cocaine had died of self-administered
overdose
compared with 36% of rats receiving heroin (Bozarth & Wise, 1985)
Cocaine passes through the placenta and
interferes with fetal development
. Cocaine-exposed children had lower birth weight, lower IQs, poorer language development, and greater distractibility (Nulman et al., 2001)

===== SLIDE 11 =====
Risks of Smoking
Because usage is more continuous with tobacco than with other drugs, the health risks are particularly high, including those for heart disease, stroke, and 17 types of cancer.
The risks result not from nicotine but rather from about 250 harmful chemicals in the smoke. At least 60 of these produce cancer, many by damaging DNA throughout the smoker’s body.
Smoking may also cause brain damage. In smokers, gray matter volume is reduced in the prefrontal cortex, anterior cingulate cortex, insula, and olfactory gyrus, with the degree of loss corresponding to the amount smoked (Fritz et al., 2014).
Smoking is the leading preventable cause of death, accounting for 480,000 premature deaths annually in the U.S. & 6 million worldwide
Recent studies have found evidence that maternal smoking impedes neuronal development in the fetus (Puga et al., 2024), and by the age of 6 to 8 years the children have reduced cortical gray matter and overall brain volume, cortical thinning in prefrontal areas, and a tendency for depression (El Marroun et al., 2013).

===== SLIDE 14 =====
Classification of Psychedelic Drugs
Garrett, Brain & Behavior, 7e. © 2027 Sage Publishing.
14
--- SMARTART ---
Serotonergic psychedelics stimulate serotonin receptors
They cause sensory distortions, repetitive geometric shapes, and intense colors and hallucinations.
EX’s: LSD, psilocybin, psilocin, and dimethyltryptamine (DMT)
LSD, psilocybin, & DMT have been used experimentally to treat psychological disorders (e.g., anxiety), with varying success
Catecholaminergic psychedelics block reuptake of dopamine, norepinephrine and serotonin
At low doses, they are psychomotor stimulants with amphetamine-like properties that increase energy, sociability, and sexual arousal; at higher doses, they produce hallucinatory effects like those of serotonergic psychedelics
EX’s: mescaline and the synthetic drug ecstasy (MDMA)
High doses or chronic use can be toxic to serotonergic neurons.
NMDA receptor antagonists inhibit the N-methyl-D-aspartate type of glutamate receptor
They are also known as
dissociatives
because they cause more intense depersonalization and a sense of unreality
EX: Ketamine, phencyclidine (PCP)
Used to be used as surgical anesthetics, but ere replaced by meds without psychedelic properties.

===== SLIDE 18 =====
Negative Effects of THC
Chronic use diminishes dopaminergic activity, which can lead to dysphoria/low mood
Long-term heavy use is correlated with various brain anomalies, including reduced volume in the hippocampus & amygdala and impaired white matter connectivity in the hippocampus and corpus callosum
There is some evidence that chronic, heavy use is associated with a drop in IQ
Prenatal marijuana exposure has been associated with behavioral problems, including impulsiveness and hyperactivity; decreased performance on visual-spatial tasks; and deficits in attention, memory, and language comprehension in adolescence
Recent studies have found evidence that maternal smoking impedes neuronal development in the fetus (Puga et al., 2024), and by the age of 6 to 8 years the children have reduced cortical gray matter and overall brain volume, cortical thinning in prefrontal areas, and a tendency for depression (El
Marroun
et al., 2013).

===== SLIDE 20 =====
Motivation for Addiction is Not Simple
Addiction is not fueled by the pleasurable effects of the drug and individuals who engage in compulsive drug taking often report a decrease in “liking” and an increase in “wanting”
Addiction is not fueled by the drug user’s desire to avoid withdrawal symptoms as the addictiveness of a drug is unrelated to the severity of its withdrawal symptoms
Some drugs have strong addictiveness but mild withdrawal (e.g., cocaine, THC)
Other drugs have strong withdrawal, but are not addictive (e.g., nasal decongestions, hypertension medications)

===== SLIDE 23 =====
Synaptic Plasticity and Addiction
Reward motivates drug taking in the early stages, but addiction involves functional and structural changes in the brain that persist after drug use ends and the drugs have cleared from the system.
These neural changes predominantly occur in the
mesocorticolimbic
dopamine system
(comprising the PFC, amygdala, hippocampus, VTA and nucleus accumbens).
Initial exposure to a substance (sometimes even just one dose) create enduring changes in these brain regions that send and receive dopaminergic projections:
Increased or decreased sensitivity to transmitter (i.e., up-regulation, don-regulation)
Increased dendrite length
Greater synaptic complexity
Hypofrontality
Exposure to addictive drugs potentiates synapses between glutamate- and dopamine-releasing neurons such that neural plasticity to drugs increases; while plasticity to natural rewards decreases.
Garrett, Brain & Behavior, 7e. © 2027 Sage Publishing.

===== SLIDE 26 =====
Detoxification and Withdrawal
The first step in quitting drug use is
detoxification
, which involves giving up the drug and allowing the body to cleanse itself of the drug and its metabolites
The
withdrawal syndrome
for different drugs vary in their intensity and danger
Withdrawal from alcohol is potentially life-threatening, with heart attacks (as part of a disorder called delirium tremens) and seizures being the main threats.
Benzodiazepine withdrawal can also trigger life-threatening seizures, and
Methamphetamine withdrawal can trigger psychosis, paranoia and suicidal thoughts.
Withdrawal is often easier to handle than the battle against repeated
relapses

===== SLIDE 31 =====
Issues Concerning Effectiveness and Acceptance of Pharmacological Treatment of Addiction
Treatment success depends on
compliance
.
Drug substitution treatment
for addiction to heroin has the strongest evidence of effectiveness, though giving a drug to combat another drug is controversial.
Pharmacological intervention increases the effectiveness of other addiction therapies dramatically.
Methadone combined with counseling produces abstinence rates of 60%–80% in heroin addiction, compared with 10%–30% for programs that rely on behavioral management alone.
Drug addiction is almost always accompanied by environmental problems and emotional issues that must be dealt with, and therapeutic benefits are greater when medication is combined with
psychosocial support
.
A major difficulty in treating addiction is
comorbidity
with personality disorders and other major mental health disorders.
This means that drug abusers have other problems that complicate their rehabilitation.
It is likely that the addiction and the comorbid disorder have a common genetic, neurological, or environmental cause.
Even when the goal of treatment is not complete abstinence, it can enable the addict to lead a healthy and productive lifestyle and avoid criminal activity.
Garrett, Brain & Behavior, 7e. © 2027 Sage Publishing.

===== SLIDE 32 =====
Novel Treatment Strategies
A novel treatment alternative is
antidrug vaccines
.
These consist of molecules that attach to the drug and stimulate the immune system to make antibodies that will degrade the drug.
A significant problem with vaccines is that they do not reduce craving, and the number of antibodies produced is limited.
No antidrug vaccine has successfully made it to market as of 2025
Anti-inflammatory treatments
have been used to ease the symptoms of withdrawal.
Miranda and Taca (2017) developed the
Bridge neurostimulation system
, a wearable acupuncture-like device that applies electrical stimulation to the ear (Figure 5.20).
The Bridge device originally was developed to treat pain,
It supposedly reduces most withdrawal symptoms within the first hour, and 97% after 5 days, and has no reported side effects.
Garrett, Brain & Behavior, 7e. © 2027 Sage Publishing.

===== SLIDE 34 =====
Separating Genetic and Environmental Influences on Addiction
The heritability of addiction was first established with alcoholism.
Children of those with late-onset alcoholism were likely to become alcoholics only if they were reared in a home with alcohol abuse.
Children of those with early-onset alcoholism were likely to become alcoholics no matter the rearing environment.
The Met158 allele of the COMT gene predisposes people to late-onset alcoholism.
Early-onset alcoholics tend to have emotional control problems.
Garrett, Brain & Behavior, 7e. © 2027 Sage Publishing.

===== SLIDE 35 =====
What Is Inherited?
(1 of 3)
About 50%–60% of addiction is due to heredity. The rest is attributable to a host of environmental factors.
Genetic influences may increase an individual’s vulnerability or provide protection.
Whether drug use will lead to addiction depends on how the individual reacts physiologically to the drug.
Impulsiveness, a major characteristic of the “addictive personality,” is about 49% heritable.
There is evidence linking impulsiveness to functional and structural brain anomalies in drug users.
Administering the amino acid KB220 has resulted in improvement in prefrontal networks, activation of the nucleus accumbens, and reduced drug craving and relapse.
Garrett, Brain & Behavior, 7e. © 2027 Sage Publishing.

===== SLIDE 36 =====
What Is Inherited?
(2 of 3)
Reduced P300 has an estimated heritability of 64%.
Alcoholics have a diminished P300 response.
Variations in dopamine genes like DRD2 (dopamine receptor D2) and gene regulators like PDE4B (regulates mood and learning) were significantly correlated with high risk of substance abuse disorders.
Genes increase or decrease transmitter levels, regulate the number and sensitivity of receptors (for both neurotransmitters and the drug itself), and control metabolism of the drug.
Drugs also change the way that genes function.
A study of smokers found more than 7,000 genes whose functioning had been modified by methylation.
Methylation is the attachment of a methyl group to DNA, which suppresses a gene’s activity.
A mother’s smoking negatively affects the genes of her unborn fetus.
Garrett, Brain & Behavior, 7e. © 2027 Sage Publishing.

===== SLIDE 38 =====
Implications of Addiction Research
The study of drug abuse and addiction has practical societal importance.
Addiction research sheds light on other kinds of vulnerability and principles of behavioral inheritance.
The fact that genetic and environmental influences operate differently in different types of alcoholism and in different cultural settings demonstrates that no behavior is simple or simply explained.
Even after we understand the relative roles of heredity and environment, there is further complexity because we must also understand the physiological mechanisms—the neurotransmitters, receptors, pathways, enzymes, and so on
The causes of addiction are many and complex.
We must look beyond simple appeals to willpower in explaining the self-defeating behavior of the addict
