===== SLIDE 21 =====
The Spinal Cord
The
spinal cord
is a finger-sized cable of neurons that carries commands from the brain to the muscles and organs, and sensory information into the brain.
The central canal inside the spinal cord circulates
cerebrospinal fluid
down its axis.
Sensory neurons enter the spinal cord through the
dorsal root
of each spinal nerve.
The cell bodies of motor neurons are located in the ventral horns.
The axons of the motor neurons pass out of the spinal cord through the
ventral root
.
Reflexes
can originate in the spinal cord.
They are simple, automatic movements in response to a sensory stimulus.

===== SLIDE 29 =====
Stages of Development in the Nervous System
The nervous system begins development when the surface of the embryo forms a
neural groove
, which curls into the
neural tube
.
Development of the nervous system then proceeds in four distinct stages:
cell proliferation
cell migration
circuit formation
circuit pruning

===== SLIDE 30 =====
Cell Proliferation
During
proliferation
, the immature cells that will become neurons divide and multiply at the rate of 250,000 new cells every minute.
Proliferation occurs in the ventricular zone, the area surrounding the hollow tube that will later become the ventricles and the central canal.
Garrett, Brain & Behavior, 7e. © 2026 Sage Publishing.

===== SLIDE 31 =====
Cell
Migration
During
migration
, newly formed neurons move from the ventricular zone to their final location.
Most of them move perpendicularly from the ventricular surface using specialized
radial glial cells
.
An overabundance of neurons migrating into the cortical layers causes it to start wrinkling and developing the sulci and gyri seen in the adult brain.
Neurons retain considerable flexibility to become a wide variety of neurons into early infancy (called
stem cells
)
.

===== SLIDE 32 =====
Circuit Formation
During
circuit formation
, the axons of developing neurons grow toward their target cells and form functional connections.
To find their way, axons form
growth cones
at their tip.
These sample the environment for directional cues.
Chemical and molecular signposts across the brain either attract or repel the advancing growth cone, guiding it along its way.
The path of the developing axon’s destination is not necessarily direct, but it is able to make direction changes along the way by changing genetic control.
Garrett, Brain & Behavior, 7e. © 2026 Sage Publishing.

===== SLIDE 33 =====
Circuit
Pruning
Circuit pruning
involves the elimination of excess neurons and synapses.
The nervous system refines its organization and corrects errors by eliminating large numbers of excessive synapses.
Synapses are strengthened or weakened depending on whether the presynaptic neuron and the postsynaptic neuron fire together.
The postsynaptic neuron sends feedback to the presynaptic terminals in the form of
neurotrophins
, chemicals that enhance the development and survival of synapses.
In the visual system, the
plasticity
(ability to be modified) of synapses decreases.
Garrett, Brain & Behavior, 7e. © 2026 Sage Publishing.
33

===== SLIDE 34 =====
Myelination
Myelination is an additional step required for full maturation of the nervous system.
It begins with the lower structures in the brain and then proceeds to the cerebral hemispheres, moving from occipital lobes to frontal lobes.
Myelination starts around the end of the third trimester of fetal development but is not complete until late adolescence or beyond.
This slow process has behavioral implications.
Garrett, Brain & Behavior, 7e. © 2026 Sage Publishing.

===== SLIDE 36 =====
How Experience Modifies the Nervous System
Stimulation continues to shape synaptic construction and reconstruction throughout the individual’s life.
Humans lose neurons as they age, but they develop more synapses, presumably as the result of experience.
Experience-induced change can involve
reorganization
, a shift in connections that changes the function of an area of the brain.
The
doctrine of specific nerve energies
claims that each sensory projection area produces its own unique experience regardless of the kind of stimulation it receives.

===== SLIDE 38 =====
Damage and Recovery in the Central Nervous System
Brain disorders create suffering and staggering financial costs.
Stroke
, also known as a cerebrovascular accident, is caused by a loss of blood flow in the brain.
Most of the damage is due to excitotoxicity, when dying neurons release excess glutamate, which overstimulates the surrounding neurons.
The neurons then die as large amounts of calcium enter the cells.
Traumatic brain injury (TBI)
is caused by an external mechanical force.
Mild traumatic injury, or concussion, is the most common TBI.

Limitations on Recovery
Regeneration
, the growth of severed axons, occurs in the PNS.
Injuries to CNS connections are permanent.
Injury leads to glial cell death and regrowth blockade through inflammatory processes.
The nervous system could also repair itself by
neurogenesis
, the birth of new neurons.
It is most extensive in the hippocampus and near the lateral ventricles, supplying the olfactory bulb.
Research suggests that if we could enhance neurogenesis, it might provide a means of self-repair for the brain.
Garrett, Brain & Behavior, 7e. © 2026 Sage Publishing.

===== SLIDE 41 =====
Compensation and Reorganization
Garrett, Brain & Behavior, 7e. © 2026 Sage Publishing.
41
--- SMARTART ---
The simplest neural recovery involves
compensation.
This is a process in which uninjured tissue takes over the functions of lost neurons.
Normally, silent side branches from other neurons in the area become active within minutes of the injury.
A more dramatic form of neural recovery involves
reorganization
of other brain areas.
Functions previously performed by damaged areas can be assumed by nearby brain areas or, in the case of massive damage, by the other hemisphere

===== SLIDE 42 =====
Possibilities for CNS Repair
Scientists are pursuing strategies for inducing self-repair.
These efforts include minimizing the initial damage, encouraging regrowth and new connections, and replacing lost neurons.
Strategies for encouraging axon regrowth are being pursued.
One option is to block receptors for the protein Nogo-A, which inhibits regeneration following injury.
Using
stem cells
to replace injured neurons is another option.
Stem cells are undifferentiated cells that can develop into specialized cells.
Stem cells in the embryo are
pluripotent
, so they can differentiate into any cell in the body.
Neural stem cells are
multipotent
, meaning they can differentiate into any neuron and glial cells.
Garrett, Brain & Behavior, 7e. © 2026 Sage Publishing.
